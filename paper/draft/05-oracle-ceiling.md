## 5. The noise floor: locating the predictability ceiling

Seven failures admit two readings. Either the challengers were the wrong ones — and a better model waits undiscovered — or there is no further signal to find, and every elaborate mechanism was mining an exhausted vein. These readings are distinguishable, because the second makes a testable claim: if the classic input set contains predictive signal beyond the weighted mean, a sufficiently flexible learner, honestly cross-validated, should recover at least some of it. We built that learner as a deliberate ceiling probe — a diagnostic, not a product — applying to political forecasting the predictability-ceiling method a mass scientific collaboration recently used to bound the forecastability of the life course (Salganik et al. 2020). Eighteen features were derived from the raw inputs, including position summaries, dispersion measures, and salience-weighted quantiles of the actor distribution; a family of models spanning linear to kernel ridge regression was fitted under seeded five-fold cross-validation with within-family selection. The clean form of the test fits the learner not to the outcome but to the *residual* the weighted mean leaves behind — the part of each outcome the mean does not already explain — and asks how much of that residual any flexible model can recover.

It recovers nothing. The cross-validated coefficient of determination on the residual is indistinguishable from zero, its bootstrap interval spanning both signs [E-REVIEW-RESIDUAL-R2]: a model granted every freedom — nonlinearity, interactions, eighteen engineered views of the data — extracts no signal beyond the influence-weighted average. The design earns its conclusion in the conservative direction. Because the weighted mean is itself one of the features, fitting the *outcome* would let ridge shrinkage bias the mean's own coefficient away from one and flatter the flexible model; fitting the *residual* shrinks toward zero — which is exactly the baseline — so any recovered signal would be real, and the offsetting optimism of in-sample hyperparameter selection cuts the other way and still finds nothing. The benchmark's resolution is finite in a way worth stating precisely: at 351 issues the smallest paired difference in mean absolute error detectable at conventional power is roughly three scale units [E-REVIEW-MDE] — a detection floor that is a property of the benchmark rather than of our analysis, and because the 351 issues already combine all three DEU waves, the benchmark cannot be enlarged, so effects below that floor are undetectable here and would remain so with more of the same data, of which there is none. The claim now rests on two independent lines of evidence that meet at the same floor. From above, the residual probe recovers nothing the mean has left. From the side, a tournament of 8 distinct solution concepts spanning the field's major traditions [E-PHASEC-COUNT] — expected-utility bargaining and its quantal-response softening, the Nash and Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solutions, probabilistic Condorcet, two fitted structural mixtures, and the weighted mean itself — ranks them so that the mean finishes first and no rival separates from it. Sections 3 and 4 are thereby reframed: the challenge model, the bargaining solutions, the Condorcet method, and the fitted blends were not defeated by a superior competitor. They were competing for signal that does not exist.

Read positively, this is a substantive claim about politics, not merely about method: once an analyst knows who is at the table, what each actor wants, how much power each holds, and how much each cares, the influence-weighted average of their positions extracts everything those facts contain about the outcome of consensual bargaining. The residual error — some twenty-three scale units — divides between measurement granularity in the outcome coding and genuine historical contingency, the portion of politics that is simply not written in positions, salience, and capability; we take up the division in Section 9. One scope condition belongs here rather than with the general limitations, because it qualifies the ceiling directly: this is a ceiling for **mean-error losses** — the mean absolute and squared errors on which the weighted mean is the optimal point estimator — not a ceiling for every loss. Section 3 showed the challenge dynamics ahead on tight hit rates; a user who scores by near-exact hits rather than by average distance is not at the mean's ceiling but on the other side of a bias–variance trade. Within mean-error losses, and within the domain and input set Section 9 delimits, the search for a cleverer aggregation of the classic inputs is, on this evidence, complete — a domain-specific instance of the broader forecasting finding that added complexity rarely earns its keep against a well-specified simple method (Green & Armstrong 2015).

Which leaves the lineage's history in need of a different explanation. If any sane aggregation of well-elicited inputs reaches the ceiling, then the celebrated performances of Policon and its descendants cannot have come from their solution dynamics. Section 6 says where they came from.
