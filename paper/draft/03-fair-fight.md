## 3. The fair fight: evaluation on expert-coded legislative bargaining

A model that claims to forecast collective decisions should be examined on the one benchmark built for exactly that claim. The Decision-Making in the European Union datasets record real legislative controversies as this model family represents them: for each contested issue, a continuum of outcomes, expert-coded actor positions and saliences, and — decisively — the outcome actually adopted. The data were assembled independently of any one model, have anchored two decades of published model comparisons (Thomson et al. 2006; Schneider et al. 2010), and are immune to hindsight contamination because the inputs were coded by domain experts against the controversy itself, not reconstructed after resolution. Our evaluation set comprises 351 scoreable issues [E-DEU-N]; the release we use is the combined third-wave dataset, which already merges all three collection waves — DEU I, II, and III, spanning 1999 to 2019 (Arregui & Perarnaud 2022) — so the benchmark's size is fixed by the field's twenty-year record, not by our sampling, and there is no earlier wave left to add. The error metric is the absolute distance between a model's forecast and the adopted outcome on the issue's own scale; and the decision criterion was pre-registered before any result was computed: the challenge model must beat two naive baselines — the capability-and-salience-weighted mean of positions, and the position of the median actor — on mean absolute error, or the gate fails.

The first evaluation handicapped the model, and we report it because the handicap is instructive. The DEU datasets record no capability as a coded variable: the exchange-model framework's third input, actor power, has always been supplied exogenously — in practice as Council voting weights — rather than elicited into the dataset, so "capability" is a modelling choice the analyst brings, not a column the experts filled. Absent a sourced table, all actors initially received equal capability — an assumption that amputates the model's defining input, power asymmetry, while quietly converting the rival baseline into the salience-weighted mean, the specification the literature already identifies as formidable (Achen 2006). Under these conditions the challenge model failed the gate [E-DEU-MAE-r1], edging the median baseline but losing clearly to the weighted mean [E-BASE-WMEAN-r1]. Two objections demanded a rematch. First, capabilities: we constructed a sourced capability table from Council voting weights under the treaty regime governing each issue, with a documented treatment of the Commission and Parliament following the dataset's own conventions, every number cited [E-METHOD-capabilities]. Second, the reference point: the original evaluation's configuration discarded the status quo entirely, yet DEU records a reference point on 261 issues, and the model family's own theory assigns it a role. The fair fight restored both, with the gate re-registered in advance: the fully equipped challenge model against the equally equipped weighted mean.

It failed again [E-DEU-GATE]. Restoring the model's genuine inputs — the power asymmetries and status-quo anchors its theory demands — did not close the gap to the simple aggregate [E-METHOD-challenge_rp vs E-METHOD-baseline_wmean], each figure pair reporting mean absolute error / root-mean-square error, and the paired difference in mean absolute error is bounded away from zero by its bootstrap interval [E-REVIEW-MAE-CI]. We state this plainly and do not soften it: the pre-registered criterion was mean absolute error, and on mean absolute error the challenge model loses. The anatomy of the failure is more informative than its size. The challenge model's errors are bimodal: its worst cases are stampedes to the extremes of the scale, forecasting one pole while the outcome sat at the other. The mechanism is visible in the model's own logic — the weighted-median dynamic allows a numerous but individually weak bloc to capture the pivotal position and drag the converged forecast to a pole, precisely the configuration in which a smooth weighted average cannot be dragged; and several of the worst misses are status-quo reversions, outcomes at the reference point that the challenge dynamics, built to simulate movement, structurally under-predict. These are not implementation artifacts: the same error signature motivates the successor search of Section 4 and reappears, as a diagnostic warning, in live use of the released software.

### The result is specific to the loss function

The pre-registered gate is settled; a reviewer's question sharpened the reading, and we report the answer as what it is — post-hoc and responsive to review, not a pre-registered result. Under mean absolute error the compromise mean wins. Under other criteria the picture changes. On *median* absolute error [E-REVIEW-MEDIAN-AE] the paired difference is smaller and its bootstrap interval spans zero [E-REVIEW-MEDIAN-CI] — by that measure the two are indistinguishable. On the hit rate — the fraction of issues forecast within five, ten, or twenty scale units of the outcome — the challenge model is *ahead* at the tight thresholds [E-REVIEW-HITS]: it is more often nearly exactly right. On the continuous ranked probability score, computed against the capability-and-salience-weighted distribution of positions, the two are essentially tied [E-REVIEW-CRPS]. And issue by issue the margin is narrow rather than a rout: the compromise mean is nearer on more issues than not, but the split does not reach significance under a sign test [E-REVIEW-WINS]. The pre-registered conclusion stands on the metric it was registered on; it does not generalise to every loss a user might care about.

The reconciling picture is a bias–variance trade, and it replaces "the challenge model loses" as this paper's characterisation of the result. The compromise mean is a low-variance, biased estimator: it never stampedes, so its errors are moderate and clustered — exactly what a squared or absolute loss rewards. The challenge dynamics are the opposite, a higher-variance, lower-bias estimator: they more often land squarely on the outcome and more often miss by a pole, a bimodal profile whose extra exact hits are bought with extra catastrophes. Under absolute-error loss the trade is bad, because the loss is dominated by the fat tail; under a hit-rate loss, which only asks whether the forecast was close, the trade is favourable. The challenge model does not lose everywhere: it trades bias for variance, and whether that trade is worth making is a property of the user's loss function, not of the model alone. What it does not do, under any loss we tested, is *beat* the mean on the criterion we pre-registered.

Two comparisons discipline the interpretation. Our error magnitudes sit squarely in the regime the published literature reports for this model family on these data — the reconstruction-era tests of the original model, and its inventor's own later comparisons (Bueno de Mesquita 2011), place it in the same range and losing to the same baseline [E-CTX-bdm2011, E-CTX-achen2006], though scoring conventions differ enough that we flag these as context rather than direct comparison. Landing where the genuine article lands is, simultaneously, the strongest external evidence for our replication's fidelity and the least flattering possible frame for the model itself: the failure we report is not a defect of our copy but a property of the original. What the failure does *not* yet establish is why the simple aggregate is so hard to beat. Section 4 reports our attempt to beat it; Section 5 reports the answer.
